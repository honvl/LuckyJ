What this book is

A table-first LuckyJ playbook.

I reviewed 1,255 unique NAGA-reviewed hanchan, 13,054 hands, and 147,337 LuckyJ discard decisions, then grouped them into habits you can use at the table.

Scope before style

Start with ordinary efficient play.

LuckyJ matches Nishiki's top discard in about 73% of eligible reviewed decisions. This book is about the other 27%, because the splits make good review questions, not because deviating is LuckyJ's default. Copy a split only when its value, safety, opponent, or placement condition is visible before the result.

Training versus test: LuckyJ learned through self-play, not by imitating Tokujou players. Tokujou was its public live test room. Tencent's description.

This book covers 1,255 unique hanchan from Apr 19–May 30, 2023: 1,079 Tokujou, 127 Upper, and 49 General. It contains no Houou or Mahjong Soul sample. Teaching examples below are restricted to Tokujou.

Transfer limit: shanten counting, yaku legality, genbutsu, and target-specific safety carry over to any room. Riichi, calling, keiten, safety-inventory, and placement thresholds have to be recalibrated for your room and point system.

Saint 3 calibration (four-player South): at an even 25,000-point finish, the current nominal placement spread is about +125 / +60 / -5 / -255 in Jade and +135 / +65 / -5 / -255 in Throne. The final-score adjustment moves those totals, but one fourth still costs roughly two firsts. So a named exit tile and late fold discipline carry over. Slow, untargeted safety hoarding does not: stronger opponents give you fewer spare turns, and lost first and second equity still counts. Jade table; Throne table (checked July 2026).

The points

The LuckyJ Operating System

LuckyJ does not play from a tile-efficiency chart alone. Efficiency is one input to a placement engine. The pattern that repeats across the corpus: decide what the hand is for, keep the routes that serve that job, hold enough defense to keep making choices, and drop a stale plan as soon as the table changes.

Decision method

How to read every discard.

1. Job

Decide what the hand is for before you look for the best shape. A hand has to matter to the table before it can buy much risk.

2. Routes

Count real routes: closed riichi, open yaku, value upgrade, safe tenpai, and clean fold.

3. Table Texture

Read the opponents' rivers and calls before trusting a raw danger number. One open hand, one riichi, and two live threats are three different games.

4. Future Danger

Price the tiles you will have to discard later. A tile that is fine now can become the discard that ends the hand.

5. Point of No Return

Decide in advance which draw, call, riichi, or second threat ends the attack. LuckyJ's aggression always has a stop condition.

6. Human Override

Check LuckyJ's third-row choices against NAGA, Mortal, and your own point arithmetic. That part of the style is the hardest to copy.

Single value honors leave early

A lone dragon or value wind with no job yet usually leaves around turn 3. When someone had already opened before the tile was cut, the child-only median was turn 6. Read that as a description of timing, not an instruction to feed the tile on turn 6. Release it while no yaku or clear advancement is visible, and choke it once advancement shows. Do not cut it by reflex on turn 1 either: about one in eight of these singletons paired before LuckyJ released them.

Pattern synthesis

What the supporting hand reviews add.

The supporting statistical and hand reviews look at LuckyJ from several angles: every East 1 start, placement-specific hands, last-place-avoidance games, and comparisons against NAGA and Mortal. They shaped the commentary, but the table logic still decides each hand. What they add up to is that LuckyJ's style is a set of switches. Hand value, hand distance, number of calls, river texture, round, and placement all change what the same tile means.

East 1 Reveals the Base Settings

With scores level and no placement pressure, LuckyJ's default target is a hand worth pushing. As a child it wants either a good-shape one-han riichi route or a hand that can reach three han or better. As dealer the value threshold drops, because the dealer turn itself is worth more. Hands below those thresholds start preserving yaku, dora, and safety instead.

Hold Safety by Shanten Count

The farther the hand is from tenpai, the more a safe tile is worth. A far hand can keep honors or visible safe material because those tiles later buy the right to call, push one more tile, or reach a safe tenpai. Being dealer or holding a very high-value hand lowers that premium.

Weak Penchans Can Be Broken

LuckyJ will break a penchan or another bad block when its best future is a riichi-only bad-shape hand. The tiles it keeps instead usually have a second job: a yakuhai pair, a honitsu or chanta route, dora use, or defense. Copy this when the bad block mostly leads to a hand you would rather not win.

Read the River Shape Around the Tile

Several reviewed hands show how much LuckyJ reacts to discard patterns and calls. It will push into one open hand, match-discard live honors early, or stop on an ordinary-looking tile when a river looks wrong. A static danger chart misses that context. The same 6m is a different tile against a normal river, a honitsu river, and a riichi declaration sequence.

Last-Place Attack Still Has Brakes

When it is fighting out of fourth, LuckyJ uses riichi pressure, keiten calls, and value upgrades more freely. The same review hands show it refusing calls or discards that would leave no safe tile after the next dangerous draw. Buy pressure when the opponent has to respect your hand, and keep one exit.

Third Row Is the Training Zone

The main warning is that LuckyJ looks less reliable in the third discard row. Sometimes it gives up tenpai equity too readily. Sometimes it is strained by multiple threats. Treat the late endgame as a separate skill. When NAGA, Mortal, and your own point calculation all disagree with a late LuckyJ move, file it as a review case rather than a lesson.

Danger Labels Need Live Context

Suji, one-chance, kansuuken, genbutsu, and early-riichi heuristics are starting points, not verdicts. Before calling a tile safe or dangerous, update the label with visible blockers, the opponent's route constraints, suit pressure, bad-shape concentration, and your own next discard.

Route Value Beats Surface Shape

The best-looking wait still has to beat the whole route. Wait shape competes with yaku certainty, value ceiling, ron likelihood, callability, future danger, and placement need. Shape is one price inside that route, not the route itself.

Inventory Comes Before Commitment

Riichi freezes your discards, so take inventory before declaring: what value you win, which dangerous tiles you are accepting, and what flexibility you lose. Calling or pushing through danger still leaves later choices, so every leftover tile needs a job: value seed, future block, safe tile, denial tile, or liability.

Synthesis rule: LuckyJ's odd-looking tiles usually have a job. Ask which switch changed: value threshold, distance versus safety, bad-block quality, opponent river, wait count, number of threats, or placement pressure.
Evidence addendum: Treat ideas from the qualitative reviews as claims to test against the replay data. The ones that held up in general form are live-context danger reading, route over shape, inventory before riichi, and continued role labeling after calls and push decisions.
01

Put Placement Before Ukeire

Check the score table before you count acceptance. Ukeire tells you which discard improves the shape. It does not tell you whether a 1,000-point win does anything, whether 5,200 changes the order, or whether a push for 8,000 mostly risks handing first place to someone else. In the South rounds the same two-shanten hand can be three different hands: one to finish cheaply, one to build toward mangan, and one to drop the moment a dangerous tile shows up.

The record behind this is plain. Over 1,255 games and 13,054 hands, LuckyJ averaged a placement of about 2.27, won about 23% of hands, and dealt in on about 10%. The split between its good and bad games matters more than the averages. In the top half of its games it averaged 3.09 wins and 0.75 deal-ins per game. In the bottom half it averaged 1.39 wins and 1.48 deal-ins. Good games came from converting the right hands and declining the wrong losses.

When LuckyJ leads, it leans toward the lower-danger tile among close candidates. That lean shows up across a little over five thousand leader spots in the mined sample, and every leader example in this chapter has at least one NAGA head on its side. Mortal support is mixed. So read the lean narrowly: when two discards are close in shape, the one that leaves an easier next turn and avoids a pointless live middle tile gets the tie. It is not a reason to choke a leading hand that still has a real route.

Urgency reverses the tie-break. From third or fourth, a 1,000-point hand that leaves the order unchanged is worth very little, and protecting shape only slows the loss. If the order needs 5,200 or 8,000, a discard that keeps riichi, dora, or mangan alive can beat the safest tile. The same logic runs the other way when a cheap win holds first: do not spend danger on a 5,200 the hanchan does not ask for.

Rule: Before counting ukeire, ask what result the hanchan needs: a cheap finish, a real value upgrade, or a fold. Then discard for that result.
Exception: When the score table is flat and one discard is much better for shape with no real danger gap, take the shape. When the order needs 5,200 or 8,000, a timid 1,000-point line is not enough.
02

Do Not Choose the Hand Too Early

In the first row, the discard with the best ukeire is usually the best discard for one version of the hand. LuckyJ tends to keep the tile that leaves several versions open: closed riichi, an open yaku hand, value through dora, or a controlled fold. Keeping those routes open costs a little acceptance now and buys a decision later, after the draws and rivers have shown which version the hand wants to be.

Five-block theory says build five blocks. The early question is which blocks deserve to stay alive. Take a constructed shape with [[2m]][[2m]][[3m]][[4m]], a [[P]][[P]] pair, red [[5pr]], and a weak [[7s]][[9s]] kanchan. Cutting the dragon pair because it is “just an honor” may smooth the shape a little, but that pair is also yaku, call permission, value, and a brake if the table turns. The kanchan is the block that needs a perfect draw to earn its place.

The data points the same way. In over 2,300 pair-or-triplet anchor spots and over 600 dora or red-five spots, LuckyJ's splits from Nishiki mostly kept the tile with a second job. Early decisions also carry the widest disagreement with NAGA, about 45%, and the late row drops to about 28%. Extra futures are worth something in the opening. By the third row most of them have expired.

Keeping options open is not a goal by itself. Once the hand is a clean ryanmen-ryanmen one-shanten, or the anchor no longer makes yaku, value, safety, or a call, cut it and take the efficiency. The error this chapter targets is breaking the pair or dumping the dora branch before you know whether the hand needs speed, value, or an exit.

Rule: Early, prefer the tile with two real jobs (shape plus yaku, value, call permission, or safety) over the tile that improves only one narrow version of the hand.
Exception: Take the efficient discard when the hand is already a clean one-shanten, when the anchor has no yaku or value, or when keeping it wrecks a strong five-block structure. Options that cannot become points, calls, or safety are clutter.
03

Call Only When the Call Changes the Hand

Opening a bad shape is not automatically giving up value. The test is whether the call turns the hand into something that can win, reach tenpai, gain tempo, or end the round. A [[P]] pon that gives yaku and a 1,000-point finish before the dealer's riichi arrives can beat waiting for a closed hand worth about two more han that may never come. A chi that turns bad two-shanten into exposed bad two-shanten pays the closed premium and gets nothing back.

LuckyJ calls often. About 36% of its hands involved a call, and it averaged about 5.65 calls per hanchan. It also passed roughly 2,000 calls that NAGA preferred, and that half of the record matters more. In top-half games it made 5.80 calls and 2.11 riichi per game, against 5.44 calls and 1.67 riichi in bottom-half games. The lesson is selection. Passing everything and taking everything both lose to opening on purpose.

Before taking the tile, look at what is true after the call. Does the hand now have a legal yaku, immediate tenpai, a real one-shanten from a dead block, a denied dealer draw, or a path to safe keiten later? Each of those shows up on the next discard. If you cannot pick the post-call discard cleanly, or cannot name the safe tile you are holding for the first threat, the call was about wanting to act.

A good closed hand should be expensive to open. Pinfu with dora, good shape, and riichi access is often a 3,900-or-better hand once riichi and ura are counted. Opening that into a fragile 1,000-point hand with no safety is impatience. Bad shapes invite calls only when the call fixes a real problem.

Rule: Call only when the call immediately creates yaku, tenpai, a real one-shanten, denial, tempo, or safe keiten equity worth more than the lost closed premium.
Exception: Pass when the closed hand has good value and shape, or when the call leaves you open, cheap, still far from tenpai, and without a safe discard for the next threat.
04

Keep Brakes on Open Hands

Calling does not remove defense from the hand. It makes defense more expensive. After a call the hand is shorter, the closed value is gone, and there are fewer draws left to find a random safe tile. Holding a genbutsu or a properly established suji against the live threat is the cost of continuing to attack without committing to throw the next live [[5p]] through a riichi.

Cheap open hands show this most clearly. A 1,000-point yakuhai hand still two-shanten cannot spend every safe tile for a few ukeire, because the push it is setting up may run into 3,900, 8,000, or a dealer. A 3,900 tenpai with a good wait can afford the same discard. Value and distance decide whether a safe tile is fuel or dead weight.

The open-hand evidence covers only hands LuckyJ had actually opened. Across 6,021 LuckyJ/Nishiki open-hand splits, LuckyJ kept a recognized defensive tile in 3,536, and 1,723 of those were tied to a live threat. These are counts of choices, not proof that keeping the tile improved the result.

After every pon or chi, ask which player the saved tile answers. A tile that is safe against the quiet player is usually dead weight. A tile that is safe against the riichi, the dealer, or the open honitsu seat is a resource. When the open hand is already a strong tenpai, or the table is quiet and the value is enough, spend the spare safety. The mistake is spending it by reflex because the hand is now “attacking.”

One saved genbutsu buys one turn, not a permanent fold. After you spend it, the next draw is a new decision. A new genbutsu or a low-danger tile that keeps the one-shanten lets you continue by mawashi. A draw that makes a good tenpai can justify a fresh push. If the only tile that keeps the shape is live against the threat, mawashi is over: push that tile on the value of the whole remaining route, or break the hand and fold.

Placement changes the price on both sides. When you are fourth and the riichi dealer is third, your win also ends the dealer turn, collects the riichi stick, and stops the gap from growing, so a strong one-shanten is worth more to keep. Dealing directly into that dealer is also the worst possible transfer. “Stop the dealer” therefore supports one tolerable continuation when the winning route is credible. It does not rescue a cheap hand that still owes a chain of live tiles.

Rule: After every call, name the tile that lets the open hand stop or continue safely. Treat the last genbutsu as a one-turn option, and reprice from zero once it is gone. If no tolerable discard keeps the hand, mawashi is over.
Exception: Spend the safe tile when the hand is already a high-value tenpai, when the tile answers the wrong opponent, or when placement requires winning now. Fourth place against a third-place dealer justifies one tolerable push from a strong one-shanten, not an unpriced series of live tiles.
05

Cut the Tile That Will Get Worse

A floater has two prices: what it accepts now and what it will cost to discard later. Most players count only the first. LuckyJ often pays the second price while the river is still quiet, cutting a useful-looking middle tile before it becomes the tile you must push through in the third row.

Take a constructed slow hand with [[2m]][[2m]], [[6p]][[8p]], [[3s]][[4s]], a lone [[5p]], [[P]][[P]], and scattered honors. If [[5p]] is not dora and pinzu is not the value plan, keeping it because it sees [[4p]] and [[6p]] is a small gain. By the time a riichi appears, that [[5p]] may be the only tile in the hand nobody has made safe.

This is one of the cleanest signals in the review. In over 3,700 spots where LuckyJ chose a lower-danger tile than Nishiki, the choice held up better than the average split. The second engine is less uniform: Mortal backs some of these cuts and prefers pure efficiency in others. So do not defend any single example as settled. The habit to copy is pricing future liability when the hand is slow and the tile has no concrete job.

Pre-cutting a middle tile is wrong when that tile is the hand. A red [[5p]], a dora connector, the tile that makes perfect one-shanten, or the only tile that makes mangan stays. Cut the future problem when it is a floater. Keep it when it is the reason the hand can fight.

Rule: In a slow hand, discard the floater whose danger will rise fastest, unless it is needed for value, yaku, or your best one-shanten shape.
Exception: Do not use this to throw dora, red fives, your strongest blocks, or the tile that makes the hand pushable. Skip it when the table is already dangerous and the floater is safer than your other options right now.
06

Buy Value While It Is Cheap

Value planning starts before the hand looks valuable. The first discard is usually the cheapest moment to keep a red-five connection, a dora side shape, or a yakuhai pair, because the hand can still reorganize around them. If you wait until one-shanten to ask for 5,200, the price is usually a block, a wait, or a dangerous tile. Efficient play is not the same as cheap play, and part of the skill is noticing when the efficient hand is too small.

The comparison is routine. A fast 1,000 or 2,000 is right when it ends a dealer turn or protects first. It is wrong when South 3 needs a mangan swing. Keeping red-five access or dora use can turn the same skeleton into 5,200 or 8,000, and that changes whether a later riichi, call, or push is worth making. As dealer the threshold moves again, because dealer hands pay one and a half times.

The data supports the narrow version of this idea. In over six hundred dora and red-five retention spots, LuckyJ kept value material that still belonged to the hand, and those keeps held up well against Nishiki. The broad version, buying risk because you are behind, did not get the same support: the showcased value trades each have a NAGA head on LuckyJ's side, but Mortal often prefers the plainer efficiency line. Copy the red and dora discipline. Do not copy the idea that an expensive tile becomes correct because you are behind.

Fake value is the trap. An isolated dora that does not connect, a yakuhai singleton you cannot use, and a honitsu plan that asks you to throw live middle tiles are not value planning. If the realistic ceiling is still 1,000 after you keep the tile, cut it and build the best hand you actually have. Feeding a dealer mangan while chasing your own is bad arithmetic.

Rule: Keep dora, red-five, and yakuhai value early when they still improve a real winning line. Do not buy value with isolated danger or decorative yaku.
Exception: Cut the value seed when it is unsupported, already too dangerous, or forces you to break the hand's best shape. Being behind raises the value you need. It does not make every risky discard profitable.
07

Make Riichi Tax the Table

Riichi is pressure as much as it is a han. The declaration adds ura chances, locks the hand, and often pushes opponents into betaori. A quiet 2,000-point damaten and a riichi hand that threatens 3,900 to 8,000 play very differently. The question is not whether you can reach. It is whether reaching makes the table pay.

LuckyJ's volume is active without being careless. It averaged about 1.9 riichi per hanchan, with more in top-half games than bottom-half games, about 2.11 against 1.67. The gap points in the right direction, but the lesson is conversion, not count. The sampled pressure riichi in this chapter get unusually broad outside support, and Mortal usually agrees with declaring. That verdict covers the declaration only, not LuckyJ's declaration discard or the wait it chose.

A good riichi changes what opponents do. Being first to threaten against quiet rivers can make a leader drop a 1,000-point open hand, freeze a dealer's two-shanten, or buy fold equity a dama hand never gets. It also moves your own threshold. A bad 1,300 reach into two live threats is usually a 1,000-point stick plus several forced danger tiles. A 5,200-class reach with a live wait can be worth the lock, because winning and making everyone fold both pay.

Riichi stops being the answer when it stops solving the problem in front of you. If dama already passes the rival in All Last, or declaring lets everyone fold when you needed direct points from one seat, stay quiet. If two opponents are already live and your wait is poor, most of the pressure lands on you.

Rule: Declare riichi when the added value and the forced reactions change the hand's EV. Stay quiet when the stick and the lock only buy a bad wait with no table pressure.
Exception: Do not riichi because the button is there. Dama is better when it already delivers the placement swing, when one specific opponent has to be hit directly, or when multiple live threats make a low-value bad wait too expensive.
08

Reprice the Whole Push Every Draw

A push is not one tile. It is every discard you are committing to make before the hand wins. Reprice that commitment after every draw, call, riichi, and new safe tile. The common leak is deciding “this is my pushing hand” once and then treating each later discard as if the table had not moved.

The review data punishes that habit. Across over 3,700 lower-danger choices against Nishiki, the splits looked much cleaner. Across over 5,000 higher-danger choices, they looked worse. That does not make every safe discard right. It means the danger reading is real information, and the second or third dangerous tile in the chain usually matters more than the first one you are staring at.

The extreme-danger sample here is nondealer-only. After removing every hand where LuckyJ was dealer and every locked discard after LuckyJ's own riichi, the Tokujou sample holds 46 chosen discards at 90% or more on the maximum NAGA danger proxy. All 46 faced at least one riichi. Thirty-three were tenpai, 12 were one-shanten, and one was farther away. Twenty came from open hands, and at least two of the three NAGA heads supported 30 of them. Repeated 99.99% readings behave like a capped alarm, not a literal deal-in probability.

The clearest supported push was a closed monster tenpai. [[3p]] read 99.99%, every NAGA head chose it, and the hand won 10 han 60 fu for +17,000. A separate third-place hand pushed [[1m]] at 95.74% to declare riichi into an existing riichi; two heads supported it, and the hand won +5,900. Those are the clean cases: real tenpai, enough reward, and broad model support before the result.

Placement urgency makes harder cases. From fourth with only 2,900 points, LuckyJ pushed [[5s]] at 99.99% while still one-shanten. It kept 13 effective tiles instead of the nine left by Nishiki's safer [[5m]], but only one NAGA head supported the push. The hand later won +6,500. That outcome shows what was bought, not that the price was right.

Calls create a different kind of extreme push: the inventory trap. With three open melds and five concealed tiles, LuckyJ pushed [[6s]] at 99.99% to keep tenpai. All three heads supported the line, and LuckyJ dealt in for -2,900. No riichi locked that hand. The earlier calls had removed most of the exits. This is why the safety count belongs before the second and third call, not after.

The strongest warning came from first place with 41,000 and five wall tiles left. LuckyJ pushed [[5m]] at 99.99% to keep an open tenpai while all three heads preferred [[7s]] at 2.97%, accepting one-shanten instead. The result was a -12,000 deal-in. Keeping tenpai explains the move. The lead, the short wall, the cheap open route, and the excellent alternative make it an overpush, not a model to copy.

Across all 46 decisions the outcomes were mixed: 12 self-wins, 11 direct deal-ins, 14 wins by another player, and nine draws. Results cannot grade the discard. Grade the state before the tile was cut: value, distance, placement, remaining wall, safe alternatives, the number of dangerous discards still ahead, and whether independent heads support the purchase.

The point comparison is blunt. Paying -1,500 at a draw can be cheaper than buying one more live turn that ends at -4,200. A hand with a realistic +8,900 finish can afford a much larger toll. The question is never whether this one tile can pass. It is whether the hand's reward covers every unsafe discard still to come.

Some nondealer hands stay on push after all that. A high-value tenpai, a riichi whose pressure and value matter, or an All Last score need can justify a dangerous tile even after the table gets worse. That is a new decision each time, not momentum from last turn. If the draw added another live middle tile to the path ahead, the old push no longer exists.

Rule: After every draw, call, riichi, and new safe tile, count the dangerous discards still ahead. Push only when that whole chain is worth more than folding now.
Exception: Keep pushing when the nondealer hand can already pay the full toll: a high-value tenpai, a meaningful pressure riichi, or a placement-critical hand that cannot fold. Even then, reprice the whole sequence instead of defending last turn's decision.
09

The Third Row Is a Counting Drill

The late row is a separate skill, not the opening with fewer tiles. Count the winning tenpai, count the safe tenpai, and count the fold. LuckyJ disagreed with NAGA on about 45% of early decisions and only about 28% of late ones. By the third row most big plans have expired, and the remaining decisions are smaller, colder, and more exact.

Keiten shows the arithmetic best. In the recorded exhaustive draws, LuckyJ was tenpai in 823 of 1,761 (46.7%). That is 823 out of all 13,054 hands, so keep the denominator straight. Noten payments are real value. They do not justify sending a live middle tile through two threats with no winning upside.

Take a constructed third-row spot. You can cut a live [[5s]] for pinfu tenpai on [[3p]][[6p]], cut genbutsu [[9s]] and stay one-shanten, or follow with [[E]] and fold. The question is not which tile looks best. Ask what the win changes, what tenpai at the draw is worth, how many safe tiles remain after this discard, and whether the next tile is also dangerous. If you have no answer for the next turn, the current push is usually not a push.

Late precision is not the same as folding everything. If the hand is a dealer mangan tenpai, the wait is alive, and the only discard is a reasonable suji, folding because “it is late” gives away too much. The discipline is separating a real attack from a vague hope: win, take safe tenpai, or fold cleanly.

Rule: In the third row, choose among three named outcomes only: winning tenpai, safe tenpai for the draw, or a real fold.
Exception: Break the rule only when placement sets a clear nonstandard objective, such as a forced last-place escape or a guaranteed-top fold. If every tenpai line needs a live center tile against strong threats and the win does not change the table, drop the hand.
10

Treat Keiten as Attack

A drawn hand is not a lost hand. Keiten is a small attack that starts after winning has become unlikely, and the payments are big enough to plan around: 1,000 when several players are tenpai, up to 3,000 when you are the only one. In South rounds that is often the same practical swing as a small win, because 1,500 or 3,000 changes who has to chase next hand.

LuckyJ reached tenpai in 46.7% of genuine exhaustive draws. That is not 46.7% of all hands. Abortive draws are excluded, and the all-hand share is about 6.3%. The last row is where safe tenpai, accepting noten, and final danger pricing decide placements. Keep a path to tenpai only when it can be walked with harmless tiles.

Harmless is the whole condition. If genbutsu, dead honors, or visibly exhausted tiles keep you tenpai, the 1,000 to 3,000 belongs in your attack calculation. If the price is a live middle tile into a dealer riichi, you are no longer buying keiten. You are risking a dealer mangan to avoid a small payment. A player who never chases draw points leaves money on the table. A player who chases them through danger has only renamed noten fear.

Forced danger ends the chase. When the only tenpai path passes two unsafe tiles, or when an open hand's value has become clear, fold and take the noten. You can still fold actively by choosing the safe discard that leaves the most future safe draws. Draw points are attack value only while the defense is still intact.

Rule: In late hands, pursue keiten while safe tiles preserve tenpai. Drop it the moment the next required discard carries real deal-in risk.
Exception: Do not chase draw tenpai through live danger. If the tenpai path needs unsafe middle tiles against riichi, dealer pressure, or a clearly expensive open hand, take the safe noten and protect the hanchan.
11

Make Engine Splits Prove Their Case

An engine disagreement is a review prompt, not a verdict. LuckyJ matches NAGA's top discard about 73% of the time, so the split hands are where the book has something to teach. Not because LuckyJ is always right, but because the split forces you to write down what the move is buying.

Keep the scale in view. The full review holds over 36,000 LuckyJ/Nishiki mismatches from about 135,000 split-head decisions, and most of them are minor. The NAGA heads are not one judge either. In the examples below, some NAGA head backs LuckyJ, and Mortal often backs Nishiki or picks a third line. Do not describe a disagreement as engine consensus when it is not.

Sort each split into one of three buckets. Blunder: the move fails basic value, safety, or yaku accounting, and you correct it. Style trade-off: LuckyJ is buying safety, pressure, or placement texture that you can explain but should not copy automatically. Genuine insight: the split shows a conditional habit you can reproduce, such as valuing a future safe exit over a little ukeire.

Point values keep the review grounded. Copying a split blindly can become a -3,900 deal-in. Flattening every LuckyJ deviation back to NAGA can cost a +2,900 win or a +1,500 draw payment. The number is not proof. It is a check: what did the move risk, what did it buy, and could a human at the table have seen that purchase?

When your explanation is thin and the stronger engines all point the other way, mark the hand uncertain and move on. The goal of review is not a higher match rate with any engine. It is copying only the decisions you can explain before the result is known.

Rule: When LuckyJ and an engine split, sort the hand as blunder, style trade-off, or genuine insight. Copy only the line whose value, risk, and table context you can explain.
Exception: Treat the split as a warning, not permission, when your explanation is thin or the engines broadly agree against the move. Fix the ordinary mistake first and leave the possible AI nuance for later study.
12

Price the Missing Yakuhai

When an opponent opens and you cannot see their yaku, a live dragon, the round wind, or their seat wind stops being an ordinary honor. It may be the tile that makes their hand legal. Discarding it offers them the pon. Holding it withholds that chance for now. The decision is about timing, not about cleanup.

There are two deliberate lines. Release the singleton while no yaku and no clear advancement are visible, accepting today's pon risk so you do not carry a worse live tile into a later turn. Once advancement shows, through more calls, a late turn, or a tenpai-looking river, choke the tile unless your own value, safety, or placement gives a concrete reason to feed it.

The observed medians describe timing only. Tiles eventually cut against an unknown open hand left around turn 8, against tanyao-shaped opens around turn 10, and around turn 7 when the yaku was already visible. Each group is conditioned on the tile surviving until that context. They do not prove that the opponent's call caused LuckyJ to wait.

Do not read this chapter as a blanket rule. The selected examples are engine-contested, and Mortal usually favors Nishiki or another line. Treat each one as a stress test. Compare the pon risk now against the danger of carrying the tile, and keep the tile when it is your pair, your value, your only safe tile, or a real honitsu or chiitoi component.

Rule: After an unclear call, mark live dragons, the round wind, and that player's seat wind as possible yaku switches. Release one early only while no yaku or clear advancement is visible. Once advancement is visible, choke it. Discarding is not denial.
Exception: Keep or even feed the yakuhai when it serves your own value or defense, when a bigger riichi makes it your only safe tile, or in South 4 when a cheap win by that specific opponent is the result you want.
13

Name Who Your Safe Tile Defends

A safe tile is safe against a particular player. Genbutsu to a quiet child does nothing against a dealer riichi. Suji to an old river matters only if that player can still punish you. LuckyJ's defensive habit is keeping a tile when you can name the opponent it answers, not hoarding safe tiles.

The profile behind this is the whole corpus. Across about 13,000 hands LuckyJ won 22.9% of hands and dealt in on 10.1%. Top-half games averaged 0.75 deal-ins and bottom-half games 1.48. That gap comes from keeping a brake for the player who matters, not from reading tiles nobody else can see.

Put prices on it. Cutting a slightly useful shape tile now costs some speed. If the kept genbutsu prevents one -4,000 turn, it has paid for that many times over. On the other side, a -500 nuisance loss is no reason to spend the only real answer to the dealer. Genbutsu is worth the most, suji is worth less, and both need an opponent's name attached before they earn a place in the hand.

Timing decides who gets the name. The keep-or-cut choice usually comes before anyone declares, and genbutsu already exists then: a player's whole earlier river becomes safe against them the moment they riichi. In child rounds LuckyJ held at least one tile from the future riichi player's river at 74.5% of 5,504 opponent declarations, and about a third of its first reaction discards were such a pre-held tile. Before a declaration, name the most likely next attacker, and seat beats river reading. The dealer riichied in 21.0% of child kyoku against 17.7% for each child, a dealer deal-in cost 6,700 points on average against 4,700, and early river features moved the riichi rate by only about 3 points. Once someone declares, the declared player replaces the dealer as the name. LuckyJ's own quiet-table discards show no dealer bias, 13.3% per turn for dealer-river tiles against 12.4% for child-river tiles, so the dealer preference is a price rule rather than a copied habit.

The label can be empty. When the table is quiet, the hand is valuable and fast, or the tile is only weak suji to someone who is not threatening you, do not tax the hand for safety that answers nobody. That tile is just a worse floater.

Rule: Keep a genbutsu or credible suji only when you can name the opponent it answers and the next bad turn it covers.
Exception: Skip the keep when the opponent is quiet, the safety is weak or aimed at the wrong player, or your hand is fast and valuable enough that the shape cost exceeds the defensive value. A safe tile to the wrong player is clutter.

Safe tiles and push-fold

Safe tiles are timing tools.

The follow-up LuckyJ, NAGA, and Mortal review narrowed the safe-tile idea. Price each safe tile by its target, its timing, and what the hand gives up by keeping it.

14

Spend Safety When Its Opponent Disappears

A tile can be genbutsu and still be the right discard. The question is not whether it was safe last turn. It is whether it protects you from the danger on the table now. When another player becomes the real threat, or the hand gets close enough that the tile blocks tenpai, yesterday's safety has expired.

This is the other half of keeping named safety. In the model-proxy audit, threat-specific keeps and spending stale or off-target safety were both less often severe Nishiki disagreements, while generic safe-tile retention was more often rejected. That is a review signal, not an outcome measure. The tile still has to show its current job.

The cost is easy to see. If an old safe tile kills a live 2,000-point tenpai chance, you have paid real points for insurance that may never pay out. If the alternative is drifting into a -1,500 noten draw, the cost is visible without any deal-in. A stale tile that answers a quiet player is shape damage, not defense.

Do not throw away your last real exit. If the tile still covers the live riichi, your hand is far, or the next draw may leave you with nothing safe, keep it. Spending stale safety is disciplined only after you can say why that tile no longer answers the dangerous player.

Rule: Whenever the main danger changes, ask who the safe tile still protects you from. If the answer is nobody relevant and it damages tenpai, spend it.
Exception: Protect the last real safe tile when it still covers the live threat, your hand is far from winning, or the next draw could force a dangerous discard with no backup. Do not call a tile stale because you want more ukeire.

Shape and route selection

Outside cuts can be shape decisions.

15

The Edge Tile Can Be the Attack

Not every terminal cut is defensive. Sometimes the outside tile is the price of keeping the center of the hand alive. A late [[1p]] or [[9s]] can be the attacking discard when it protects the middle connector that actually reaches tenpai, while also lowering the danger of the discard itself.

Take a constructed excess shape like [[1p]][[3p]][[4p]][[6p]][[7p]] in a hand that already has value. Cutting [[1p]] leaves [[3p]][[4p]] and [[6p]][[7p]], two normal ryanmen cores. Cutting [[4p]] because [[1p]] looks safer keeps a dead-looking outside tile and damages the part of the hand that wins. In five-block terms, the terminal is not a block. The middle tiles are.

The pattern is common. In over 5,000 spots LuckyJ cut outside material while keeping the inside line, and the reverse pattern appeared in over 10,000 spots. Those are counts of model splits, not proof that the outside cut is better. Mortal support in the showcase is mixed. Copy the structure only when the outside tile has a weaker job than the middle connector it protects.

Terminals and outside tiles are not free discards. A [[1m]] pair can be chiitoi material, a [[9p]] can be dora, and an outside tile may be your only genbutsu against a dealer riichi. When the outside tile is value, pair, yaku, or target-specific safety, it is no longer the cheap tile. The principle applies when the edge really is the cost of keeping the winning center.

Rule: When choosing between an outside tile and a middle connector, keep the connector if it is the tile that makes your real tenpai or win possible.
Exception: Do not cut the outside tile when it is dora, a valuable pair, part of chanta, ittsu, or sanshoku, or your only safe tile against the live threat. Drop the idea when the middle connector is already dead, redundant, or too dangerous to use next.

Calls, honors, and opponent yaku conditions

Honor tiles need role labels before you judge them.

16

Label Every Honor Before You Move It

“Honor tile” is not a strategic category. A dragon pair in your hand, a live dragon for an open opponent, a wind with two copies visible, and a genbutsu honor against riichi are four different objects. Review them all as “honors” and you will cut value tiles too early and keep dangerous tiles too long. The first step is naming what the tile is doing right now.

LuckyJ's timing split shows why the label matters. Loose self-yakuhai with no open hand around were cleared at a median of turn 4. In tanyao-shaped open contexts they waited until around turn 10. That is a role change, not a contradiction. The same [[C]] can be a bad early singleton, a future 1-han pair, the opponent's missing yaku, or your last honest fold tile.

Use four labels. Self value: your yakuhai pair, honitsu material, a chiitoi pair, or a dora-connected plan. Opponent condition: live dragons and value winds that could complete an unclear open hand. Dead tile: an honor with little value and enough copies visible that nobody can use it. Defense: genbutsu, multi-player safety, or a low-friction tile reserved for the first riichi. A self-value dragon can make 1,000 by itself and 3,900 with dora. An opponent-condition dragon can cost you the round.

Labels expire. A lone guest wind is fine early and gets worse late against chanta, kokushi, or toitoi shadows. A dead honor becomes precious when it is your only safe tile against a dealer riichi. Relabel after every call, riichi, visible copy, and score change, then cut or keep the honor for the role it has now.

Rule: Before cutting or keeping an honor, label it as self value, opponent yaku condition, dead material, or defense, then play the rule for that label.
Exception: Do not play an old label after the table changes. Keep a “loose” honor when it becomes your only safe tile, and cut a “safe” honor when it becomes an opponent's live yaku condition.

Practice system

How to train this style

Turn-one job naming

Before every first discard, write one phrase: quick end, value target, open rush, safety hedge, or last-place attack.

Three-route bad-hand drill

For every bad hand, mark the best tile for tanyao, yakuhai, and safety. Discard the tile that contributes least across all three.

Dangerous floater audit

On turn five, find the tile you least want to discard into a riichi. If your hand is slow, cut it now.

Call purpose

Every call needs a stated purpose: tenpai, a real one-shanten, yaku, an ippatsu break, or denial. No purpose, no call.

Opponent yaku-condition audit

After every opponent call, mark dragons, round wind, and that player's seat wind. When their yaku is unclear, clean loose yakuhai before it becomes their yaku condition.

River-safe inventory

Before the middle row, mark every tile in your hand that is already in an opponent river or suji to it. Spend the last safe tile deliberately.

Future push path

Before pushing, name every dangerous tile that must pass before you win. If there are two or more, downgrade the hand sharply.

Draw-tenpai defense

When folding, prefer safe discards that preserve harmless tenpai routes. Count noten-bappu as a real objective.

Penchan autopsy

When you break a penchan, write why: bad riichi-only future, value route, safety route, or river danger. If none applies, keep the block.

Third-row replay

Review every third-row push or fold with NAGA, Mortal, and your own point calculation. This is where copying LuckyJ without checking costs the most.
