Where your points go against LuckyJ.

This guide measures 80 of your Jade South games since January 2026, 830 hands, with the same code that measured 1,255 LuckyJ games. It keeps the lessons from our reviews and shows your own table at each turn where a different tile, call or declaration would have played closer to LuckyJ.

Most of your game already matches. Tenpai speed, tile efficiency, call rate and honor timing are LuckyJ's numbers, and you fold against a riichi at least as well as LuckyJ does. The gap is in which hands you finish and what they are worth, plus a small set of tile choices once someone has called.

Your first rate since January, 23.8%, sits within one standard error of your career 26.3% over 3,877 games. The Jade players at your tables win, deal in and score like LuckyJ's Tokujou opponents. The difference that is real is LuckyJ's 31.7% firsts against your career 26.3%, and it lives in the value of your wins.

How to read the tables

Your seat is at the bottom.

Each table is rebuilt from the game record at the moment you had drawn and were about to discard. The tile you drew sits apart at the right end of your hand. Every example lists the tile you cut, the better cut, and every other discard with the shanten it leaves, how many tiles improve it, and how safe it was against each player who had declared riichi or called.

Your cut the tile you cut Better the better cut

Safety labels read from your seat at that turn. genbutsu is in that player's river, or was discarded by anyone after their riichi without being ronned; those tiles also anchor suji. suji is a 1, 2 or 3 (or 7, 8 or 9) whose partner three away is anchored. nakasuji is a 4, 5 or 6 with both partners anchored; with only one it is half suji, and still mostly live. A 4 whose 1 is anchored, or a 6 whose 9 is, reads as virtual nakasuji when that player also cut the 5 early: the early 5 is the sotogawa read that covers the other side. An honor with two other copies showing can only be hit by a tanki. Everything else is live.

On hands with no dora you already play like LuckyJ, and you win them slightly more often. On deals with one dora or more, LuckyJ makes 8.8 mangan per 100 hands and you make 3.9; on deals with none, 4.1 and 3.8. Dora reach your hand at the same rate and you both cut about a fifth of them, so the gap comes from three habits.

Riichi at the first closed tenpai. With nobody in riichi, LuckyJ declares 51% of first tenpais on 3 or fewer live tiles, 82% on 4 to 7 and 93% on 8 or more. You declare 41%, 76% and 89%. Your 20 dama wins average 5,880 and 20% reach mangan; LuckyJ's average 7,667 and 36% reach mangan, because it keeps dama mostly for hands that are already big.

Shape yaku on top of the dora. LuckyJ wins 5.0 tanyao hands per 100 and you win 3.0; honitsu 2.1 against 1.3. In deals with lone honors and isolated terminals, LuckyJ has cleared every terminal and honor by turn 6 in 35% of hands and wins 28% of those deals. You clear 22% and win 17%. Junk goes first: guest winds and isolated terminals on turn 1, the lone value honor by turn 3 or 4.

A yakuhai pon needs something behind it. When your first call is a yakuhai pon, 47% of the time the hand has no dora (LuckyJ 41%). Your pon hands with two or more dora win 28% of the time against LuckyJ's 43%, though that is only 18 of your hands. A bare yakuhai pon from 3-shanten earns LuckyJ about 16 points per hand, which is nothing. Let the first copy go when the pon would leave you 3-shanten with no dora, unless you lead or the hand has a second plan such as toitoi or honitsu.

Rule Count the dora and red fives on turn 1. None: a cheap hand to finish if it comes and fold first when someone attacks. One: a mangan candidate, so riichi at the first tenpai and build tanyao when the deal allows. Two or more: the hand of the game, so call when the call lands you at 1-shanten or tenpai, riichi at the first closed tenpai and push it at tenpai.
Exception Dama is right when the hand is already mangan without riichi and the wait is poor, or on a tenpai from turn 11 on when you lead in the South round. Ask again every draw: LuckyJ declares later in 17% of the tenpais it first passes on, you in 10%.

Chapter three · Calls · added 22 September

Against a call, take the safe tile that costs nothing

Folding against one call does not mean destroying the hand, and your choices there already match LuckyJ's. When a safe tile is also the most efficient tile, you take it 61% of the time and LuckyJ 56%. You pay acceptance for safety 14% of the time and break a shanten for it about 3% of the time, the same as LuckyJ.

LuckyJ's pattern: at tenpai it takes a free safe tile 93% of the time, and from turn 13 on it takes one 79 to 97% of the time at any shanten. It pays one or two tiles of acceptance for safety 26% of the time, rising to 35% once the caller has cut three or more tiles from hand since the call and 42% from turn 13. Those are the moments live tiles start to hit: in LuckyJ's games a live tile hit a caller with three such cuts behind them 1.9 times per 100, against about 0.5 before that, and 2.4 times from six turns after the call against 0.25 right after it. It breaks a shanten only from 1- or 2-shanten after turn 12, and then 21 to 24% of the time. Against two calls it is stricter: from turn 9 it cuts a live tile only about 30% of the time when a safe tile keeps its shanten.

Your live tiles still get hit more than twice as often against a single call, 1.4% of live cuts against LuckyJ's 0.64%, and most of those were pushes LuckyJ would also make. The examples below are the ones that were not: the safe tile cost nothing, cost a tile or two against a two-call hand, or the river had already said what the hand was.

Rule Against one call, play for efficiency. When two tiles are equal, cut the safe one once the caller could be ready, from about their tenth discard; before that, cut an equal live honor or terminal and keep the safe tile, as chapter 13 explains. Once the caller has cut three or more tiles from hand since the call, or from turn 13, a tile or two of acceptance for a safe tile is often worth paying. Break a shanten for safety only from 1- or 2-shanten after turn 12.
Exception Against two calls, a value pon with a second call, or a river that has named its suit, pay more. Those hands are close to tenpai and their waits are easier to guess.

Evidence 1,594 of your discards and 24,618 of LuckyJ's where exactly one opponent had exactly one open meld and nobody was in riichi.

Chapter five · Push · added 22 September

Push the tenpai that can win

At tenpai against a riichi you push 38% of the time and LuckyJ 43%; from turn 13 on, 26% against 35%. The clearest gap is the open tenpai whose wait can only be kept with a dangerous tile: LuckyJ pushes it 84% of the time and you 71%, over 63 of your hands. LuckyJ pushes 93% of those with 8 or more live tiles and 76% with 3 or fewer.

The yaku decides the push more than the wait width does. With a yaku on the wait, LuckyJ pushes an open tenpai 68% of the time even on 2 live tiles and 95% on 8 or more. With no yaku on any wait it pushes 55% of them, since such a hand cannot win by ron. A closed tenpai with no yaku can only win by tsumo, and on one live tile that almost never happens.

Rule Push a tenpai that has a yaku, even on a narrow wait, and keep an 8-tile wait even when it costs a red five. Play a tenpai with no yaku the way you would play 1-shanten.
Exception The closest call in LuckyJ's data is an open tenpai with no yaku on any wait, pushed 55% of the time. With a yaku and a live wait it pushes at least 68% of the time, and 95% with 8 or more live tiles.
